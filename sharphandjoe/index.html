Bootleg Toy Files

Sharp Hand Joe

The knockoff Freddy Krueger who got his own name, his own memes, and a cult following.

So who is Sharp Hand Joe?

Picture a guy in a fedora and a striped sweater with a razor-fingered glove on one hand. Now change the sweater to red and white, give him blue pants, and sell him in a blister pack with a graffiti-style logo at a drugstore. That is Sharp Hand Joe: an unlicensed action figure that borrows heavily from Freddy Krueger of A Nightmare on Elm Street.

He was never a movie character. He is a cheap 1990s toy that collectors, YouTubers and meme-makers decided was too weird to forget.

Quick Facts

What
An unlicensed (“bootleg” or “KO”) Freddy Krueger action figure
Toy line
“Monster” by Sungold, with a German release by Simba Toys as “Monster Vampire”
Era
The 1990s. The cards carry no year, so collectors only know it as early-to-mid 90s
Look
Red-and-white striped sweater, fedora, blue pants, claw glove on his right hand
Where it sold
Supermarkets, pharmacies and discount stores rather than toy stores
Today
A carded one sold for $90 on eBay; a loose figure listed at about £70

The man he's copying

Freddy Krueger arrived in A Nightmare on Elm Street in 1984, a dream-invading killer with a hat, a glove of razor blades, a red-and-green striped sweater and a steady supply of one-liners. By the late 80s he was everywhere: licensed merchandise, plus a flood of unofficial masks, toys and knockoffs that turned up in discount aisles around the world.

Sharp Hand Joe is one of the best-remembered members of that second group. The sweater colors are off, the face is friendlier than it has any right to be, and that is exactly why people love him.

Meet the Monster line

Joe came from Sungold's Monster line, a set of six figures sold on bright, dripping-slime cards. Most of the cast were knockoffs of the classic Universal Monsters: a mummy, a Frankenstein, a werewolf and a vampire. The others were an orange-haired figure who looks a lot like the Toxic Avenger, and then Joe, who didn't fit with any of them.

Every figure has the same simple moves: both arms, both legs, and a head that turns side to side. One collector who tracked down the full set points out that some of the arms look copied from the old Marx monster toys, and that the vampire and Frankenstein resemble figures from the Toxic Crusaders line.

Where did the name come from?

This is the part that surprises most people. The original Sungold cards never named any of the characters. The names came from Simba Toys in Germany, who sold the same figures as Monster Vampire and printed names on the back of the card: Bloody Mummy, Killing Beast, Midnight Wolf, Green Frankie, King of Vampires, and Sharp Hand Joe.

So, according to collectors, the internet's favorite fake Freddy got his name from a German toy card, and the rest of the world has been repeating it ever since.

The variants

There is more than one Sharp Hand Joe. Collectors usually sort them like this:

  • Sungold “Monster” (the original). The standard carded figure, with no character names on the card. One collector wiki notes the claw glove has separate fingers on this release.
  • Simba “Monster Vampire.” The German release with a different logo, slightly changed sculpts, and the character names on the back. The same wiki says the glove is a single piece here.
  • Sungold Mini-Monster twin packs. A smaller, UK-only set of six two-packs with twelve figures. Its Freddy knockoff wears a yellow-and-red sweater, green pants and a bright blue hat, and I-Mockery notes a facial tattoo.

Why collectors love him

Part of it is the packaging. These were cheap impulse gifts from drugstore racks, never meant to be collectibles, and one toy blogger described the card art as looking like a Creepshow and Double Dragon crossover.

The biggest part is the internet. Video reviewers like Phelous covered the Monster line in 2015 (“Bootleg Zones”), and the name did the rest. He has been made into T-shirts, fan films and endless memes, including “Cotton Eyed Joe” remixes with Sharp Hand Joe in the title role. Entertainment sites have run explainers on how a throwaway toy became a legend.

What nobody seems to have documented: the exact year, the designer, or how many were made. If you know, tell the collectors.
